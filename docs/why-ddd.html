    Why DDD?

    What problem domain-driven design solves, what it costs, and how to tell whether your project needs it.

    The problem: essential complexity

    Fred Brooks split software complexity into accidental complexity, which comes from our tools and choices, and essential complexity, which comes from the problem itself. Frameworks, clouds and libraries keep shrinking the accidental part. Nothing shrinks the essential part. If the rules for refunding a partially used season ticket are intricate, the code that implements them will be intricate too.

    Domain-driven design, introduced in Eric Evans' 2003 book Domain-Driven Design: Tackling Complexity in the Heart of Software, is a set of practices for handling essential complexity on purpose instead of by accident.

    Symptoms DDD addresses

    SymptomWhat's usually going onDDD response
    Developers and the business talk past each otherTwo vocabularies, translated in people's heads (lossily)Ubiquitous language
    A "simple" change touches 30 files across the codebaseNo boundaries; every concept is shared by everythingBounded contexts
    The Customer class has 80 fields and nobody knows which are used whereOne model trying to serve every departmentBounded contexts, context maps
    Business rules are scattered across controllers, services, SQL and the UIAnemic objects plus procedural "service" scriptsRich domain model, aggregates
    Invalid states show up in production dataNothing enforces invariants in one placeValue objects and aggregates that make illegal states unrepresentable
    Engineering effort spreads evenly, so the differentiator gets no more care than the login pageNo sense of what matters mostCore domain distillation

    What DDD is not

    • Not a framework or architecture. There's nothing to install. Hexagonal architecture, CQRS and microservices pair well with DDD, but they are separate ideas.
    • Not "entities, repositories and services" folders. That is sometimes called DDD-lite: the tactical patterns without the strategic thinking. It brings most of the ceremony and few of the benefits.
    • Not a big up-front design. Modelling is continuous. You expect your understanding, and so the model, to deepen over time.
    • Not microservices. Bounded contexts often make good service boundaries, but a well-modularised monolith is a perfectly good home for several contexts.

    When it pays off, and when it doesn't

    Good fit

    • Rich business rules that change often
    • The software is the competitive advantage
    • Access to domain experts who will talk to you
    • A long-lived system with several teams
    • Concepts mean different things to different departments

    Poor fit

    • CRUD over a database with little logic (forms in, rows out)
    • A throwaway prototype or a short-lived tool
    • A purely technical problem (a compiler, a cache, a codec)
    • A generic capability you could buy (billing, auth, email)
    • No one available who understands the business

    A reasonable rule of thumb: strategic DDD is almost always worth it. Talking to experts, naming things well, and drawing boundaries are cheap and help any non-trivial system. Tactical DDD is worth it where the logic is complex, typically in the core subdomain. Elsewhere, a simple transaction script or CRUD module is a legitimate, even preferable, choice.

    Cost

    DDD takes time with domain experts, discipline about boundaries, and more types (value objects, events, ports) than a naive design. Those costs are real. They pay back when the domain is complicated and the system will live for years. They don't pay back for a weekend project.

    The core loop

    Most of DDD comes down to a loop you repeat for the life of the system:

    flowchart LR
  A["Talk with domain experts<br/>using concrete scenarios"] --> B["Refine the language<br/>& find boundaries"]
  B --> C["Express the model<br/>in code"]
  C --> D["Code & tests expose<br/>awkward spots"]
  D --> A

    When the code feels awkward (a method that needs six flags, a class nobody can name), that is often a sign the model is missing a concept. Evans calls the moment you find it a breakthrough: a refactoring toward deeper insight that simplifies everything around it.
