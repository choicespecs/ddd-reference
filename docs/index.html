    Domain-Driven Design, explained

    A learning reference for domain-driven design (DDD). It covers the strategic ideas that shape a system, the tactical patterns that shape the code, and the judgement calls in between.

    What this is

    Domain-driven design is a way of building software for complicated businesses. It asks you to put the business problem, the domain, at the centre of the design. You build a shared language with the people who know the domain best, then write that language directly into the code.

    This site is a reference, not a framework or a starter template. It explains each idea in plain terms, shows a small code example (mostly Java, but the ideas are language-agnostic), and points out the trade-offs and the common ways each idea goes wrong. One fictional business, an event-ticketing company called Fieldhouse, runs through every chapter so the examples connect.

    How to read it

    New to DDD? Read the Start and Strategic design sections in order before the patterns. Most failed DDD efforts skip straight to entities and repositories and miss the part that matters. If you already know the basics, use the search box (press /) or jump to the checklist.

    The big picture

    DDD has two halves. Strategic design decides what to build and where the boundaries go. Tactical design is a toolkit for building the model inside one of those boundaries.

    DDD in ten sentences

    1. Software complexity mostly comes from the domain, not the technology, so design effort should go there.
    2. Developers and domain experts build a ubiquitous language together and use it everywhere, including in the code.
    3. A business is made of subdomains. Only a few are your competitive edge (the core), and they deserve your best modelling.
    4. A single model for the whole business becomes contradictory, so each model lives inside a bounded context where its terms have one meaning.
    5. Contexts must collaborate. A context map makes their relationships, and the team politics behind them, explicit.
    6. Inside a context, value objects capture concepts defined by their attributes, and entities capture things with a lasting identity.
    7. Aggregates group objects that must stay consistent together and are changed only through their root, one per transaction.
    8. Domain events record facts the business cares about and let other aggregates and contexts react without coupling.
    9. Keep the domain model free of infrastructure. Persistence, messaging and UI depend on the domain, never the other way round.
    10. The model is never finished. Refactor it whenever you learn something deeper about the domain.
