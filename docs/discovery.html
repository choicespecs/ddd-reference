    Strategic design

    Discovering the domain

    Collaborative workshops that get domain knowledge out of experts' heads and onto a wall, where everyone can see the gaps.

    Why workshops beat interviews

    Domain knowledge is spread across people, and much of it is tacit: the expert does it but has never had to explain it. Interviews give you each person's partial view one at a time. A shared visual workshop puts the whole flow in front of everyone, and the disagreements surface right away. "Wait, Finance thinks refunds happen before cancellation?"

    Event Storming

    Invented by Alberto Brandolini. You model a business process as a timeline of domain events (past-tense facts) on sticky notes across a long wall. It has three common levels:

    LevelPurposeOutput
    Big PictureExplore a whole business line with many stakeholdersShared understanding, hotspots, candidate subdomains and boundaries
    Process ModellingDetail one process: who does what, which rules applyCommands, policies, read models, external systems
    Software DesignDesign the model for one contextAggregates, their commands and events

    The sticky-note grammar

    LilacPolicy ("whenever X, then Y")Whenever a hold expires, release its seats

    Hotspots are the point

    The red stickies are the most valuable output. "Payment succeeded after the hold expired" is a real edge case that becomes a business decision: auto-refund, or re-hold if the seats are still free. It's far cheaper to find on a wall than in production.

    Finding boundaries on the wall

    • Where the language shifts ("order" becomes "ticket" becomes "admission")
    • Where different actors or departments take over
    • Where a pivotal event marks a phase change (Order Confirmed separates selling from admitting)
    • Where timing decouples naturally (seconds during an on-sale vs. days until the event)

    Domain Storytelling

    Developed by Stefan Hofer and Henning Schwentner. A domain expert tells a concrete story ("Last Friday, Priya bought two seats…") while a moderator draws it as pictographic sentences: actor → activity → work object → to actor, numbered in order. It's good for getting at specific, real cases and for spotting where work objects change name.

    Example Mapping

    From Matt Wynne (Cucumber). For one user story, list its rules (blue cards), give each rule concrete examples (green), and park questions (red). The examples become your test cases almost word for word, which gives a direct line from conversation to tests.

    Story: Buyer places a hold ├─ Rule: can't exceed purchase limit (6) │ ├─ Example: owns 4, holds 2 → ok │ └─ Example: owns 4, holds 3 → rejected: "limit is 6, you already have 4" ├─ Rule: a seat can only be held by one buyer │ └─ Example: A holds 12F; B tries 12F → rejected └─ Question: do refunded tickets count toward the limit?

    Facilitation tips

    • Get the right people in the room: people who do the work, not only managers who describe it.
    • Use an unlimited modelling surface, either a long paper roll or an online whiteboard.
    • Start chaotic (everyone writes events silently), then enforce the timeline.
    • Don't resolve every disagreement on the spot. Mark it as a hotspot and move on.
    • Photograph or export the result, but remember the shared understanding matters more than the artifact.
