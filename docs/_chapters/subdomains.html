--- title: Subdomains lede: Breaking the business into parts, then deciding which parts deserve your best people and most careful modelling. ---

Problem space vs. solution space

A domain is the area of business you're building software for, such as live-event ticketing. A subdomain is a coherent part of that business: on-sale fairness, payments, door admission. Subdomains describe the problem space. They exist whether or not any software does.

Bounded contexts belong to the solution space. They are the boundaries you choose for models and code. Ideally each subdomain maps to one bounded context, but they're distinct ideas and they don't always line up.

The key question

"If we did this exactly as well as our competitors, would we lose anything?" If the answer is no, it isn't core. Spend the modelling effort somewhere else.

Matching investment to type

```mermaid quadrantChart title Where to invest modelling effort x-axis Low differentiation --> High differentiation y-axis Low complexity --> High complexity quadrant-1 Core - model deeply quadrant-2 Generic - buy it quadrant-3 Supporting - keep simple quadrant-4 Watch for change Sales: [0.85, 0.85] Payments: [0.15, 0.75] Notifications: [0.1, 0.3] Event Planning: [0.4, 0.35] Admission: [0.35, 0.5] ```
  • Core: rich domain model, aggregates, thorough tests, constant refinement with experts. This is where DDD's tactical patterns earn their cost.
  • Supporting: the simplest thing that works. A CRUD module with validation is fine. Don't build aggregates and event buses for a venue-address form.
  • Generic: integrate a product (a payment provider, an email service) and isolate it behind an anti-corruption layer so it can't distort your model.

Distilling the core

Evans calls the work of separating the core from everything else distillation. Useful techniques:

  • Domain vision statement: one page saying what the core domain is and why it matters. Example: "Fieldhouse wins organisers because on-sales are fair to fans: no overselling, no bots hoarding seats, and released seats get back on sale within seconds."
  • Highlighted core: mark the core modules explicitly so newcomers know where the important code is.
  • Segregated core: move supporting logic out of the core modules so the core stays small and readable.
  • Generic subdomain extraction: when you notice a generic concern (time zones, money, address formats) tangled in the core, pull it out into a library or service.

Classifications change

A subdomain's type isn't permanent. A supporting subdomain can become core when the strategy shifts. If Fieldhouse decided to compete on "fastest door entry", Admission would become core. A core subdomain can also become generic once the market catches up. Revisit the classification when the business strategy changes.
